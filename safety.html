How to find them

Foraging safety & ethics

This page is the one to read before a walk in the woods. The rules are conservative on purpose. Wild food is not worth a poisoning, a fine, or a damaged patch.

Never eat it unless you are 100% sure

If identification is not certain, do not eat the plant or mushroom. “Pretty sure,” “it looks like the picture,” or “a friend ate something similar” is not enough. This site is not a complete field guide and is not a substitute for in-person teaching or a trusted regional book.

Identification

  • Use several features at once: habitat, season, the whole plant or mushroom (cap, pores or gills, stem, base, flesh, smell), not a single color or a phone app guess.
  • Learn the lookalikes of every species you intend to eat. For morels, that includes false morels. For chanterelles, jack-o’-lanterns. For ramps and wild onion, toxic lily-family plants that do not smell of onion.
  • Do not taste plants or mushrooms as an identification method.
  • The onion-or-garlic smell of a crushed Allium is required, but it is not a complete identification by itself.
  • When you cut a morel lengthwise, a true morel is hollow from cap through stem. If that test fails, do not eat it.
  • Cook wild mushrooms thoroughly. Do not eat them raw. Try a small amount the first time, even when the identification is certain — edible species still cause stomach upset in some people.
  • If someone may have been poisoned, contact poison control or emergency services. Do not wait it out. In the United States, Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222.

Permission and land rules

  • Do not trespass. On private land, get the landowner’s permission first.
  • Great Smoky Mountains National Park generally prohibits collecting plants, mushrooms, and other natural items. A beautiful park woods is not a foraging ground.
  • Most Tennessee state parks also restrict or prohibit taking plants and fungi. Rules vary by park and can change; read the current rules for the exact place you are going.
  • Some national forest and other public lands allow limited personal-use collecting, and some do not. Protected plants, research areas, and wildlife refuges often have tighter rules. Check with the land manager before you pick anything.
  • Roadside and industrial edges can be contaminated or sprayed. Passing up a convenient plant is reasonable.

Sustainability

  • Take only what you will use. Leave plenty behind for the plant or fungus, for wildlife, and for other people.
  • Ramps are a special case: they grow slowly, and heavy digging has damaged patches across the Appalachians. If you harvest at all, prefer a few leaves from a large, healthy stand, and leave the bulbs in the ground.
  • For mushrooms, do not rake the forest floor or tear up logs to “find more.” Cut or pinch what you take and leave immature or worm-eaten specimens.
  • Do not strip a berry patch. Birds and other animals use that fruit.
  • Know whether a species is uncommon, slow-growing, or under pressure in your county, and leave those plants alone.

In the kitchen

  • Keep a sample of a new mushroom in the refrigerator, or a clear photograph of the whole specimen, until you know a meal sat well.
  • Do not mix a lot of different wild mushrooms in a first meal. If something goes wrong, you will want to know which species was eaten.
  • Follow tested instructions for canning and curing. See Processing wild foods for traditional methods and their limits, including the risk of botulism in low-acid jars and in homemade oil infusions.
