In the kitchen

Recipes

Short, practical cooking for wild ingredients you can find in the Southeast. Use only food you have identified with certainty, harvested legally, and — for mushrooms — cooked thoroughly. These are starting points, not restaurant recipes.

A caution before you cook. If there is any doubt about a plant or mushroom, do not eat it. People can also react to edible species; try a small amount the first time. This is not medical or foraging advice.

Spring · Serves 2

Skillet morels on toast

Morels have a short season in East Tennessee, usually in the weeks when redbuds and mayapples are out. Once you are sure of the identification, cooking them simply is enough. Always split them and cook them through; raw morels should not be eaten.

Ingredients

  • A generous handful of fresh morels, halved lengthwise and checked for debris
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • A pinch of salt, and black pepper if you like
  • A small minced shallot, or a few ramp leaves if you have them
  • Two thick slices of toast
  • A squeeze of lemon at the end (optional)
  1. Soak briefly only if they are gritty, then pat very dry. Discard any that are rotting or unidentifiable.
  2. Melt the butter in a skillet over medium heat. Soften the shallot or ramp leaves.
  3. Add the morels in a single layer. Cook, stirring now and then, until they have given off moisture and then browned, usually 8–12 minutes. They should be tender and fully cooked, not rubbery and wet.
  4. Season, pile onto toast, and add lemon if you want brightness.

From the field: How to find morels · Lookalikes include false morels, which can be dangerous. See Safety & ethics.

Spring · About 1 cup

Ramp leaf butter

Ramps grow slowly, and digging whole plants from a small patch is hard on a population. This butter is written for the leaves only. It is a traditional way to make a modest harvest last: spread it on bread, melt it over potatoes, or finish a steak.

Ingredients

  • 1 stick (8 tablespoons) unsalted butter, soft
  • A packed handful of well-washed ramp leaves, very finely chopped
  • A pinch of salt
  • A little lemon zest, optional
  1. Dry the leaves thoroughly after washing so water does not sit in the butter.
  2. Chop them almost to a paste. Work them into the butter with salt and zest until the color is even and flecked with green.
  3. Roll in wax paper or pack into a small jar. Refrigerate and use within a week, or freeze in small portions.

From the field: How to find ramps · Leaves also take well to vinegar pickling when you want to put some away.

Summer–fall · Serves 2–3

Pan-seared chicken of the woods

Young, moist, flexible edges are the part to cook. Older shelves can be sour, dry, or full of insects and should be left. Some people get an upset stomach from this mushroom, even when it is correctly identified, so cook it thoroughly and try a small portion first.

Ingredients

  • About ¾ pound young chicken of the woods, cleaned and sliced ½-inch thick
  • Salt and pepper
  • 2 tablespoons oil or butter, or a mix
  • A smashed clove of garlic, or wild onion greens if you have a sure identification
  • Parsley or a squeeze of lemon, to finish
  1. Trim away tough, woody, or insect-damaged parts. Pat the slices dry so they will brown.
  2. Season, then sear in a moderately hot skillet in a single layer. Cook several minutes per side, until browned and cooked through, often 10–15 minutes total. If the pieces are thick, lower the heat and give them more time.
  3. Add garlic only for the last minute so it does not burn. Finish with lemon or parsley.

From the field: How to find chicken of the woods. Leftovers can be sliced and dried if they were young and clean.

September–October · Serves 4–6

Simple pawpaw pudding

Pawpaws turn overripe almost as soon as they are perfect. This baked pudding is a straightforward way to use pulp from fruit that is soft, fragrant, and fully ripe. Do not eat the seeds or skin, and do not use hard, unripe fruit. Some people are sensitive to pawpaw; try a little plain pulp first.

Ingredients

  • About 2 cups seeded pawpaw pulp (from several ripe fruits)
  • 3 eggs
  • 1 cup milk or half-and-half
  • ⅓ to ½ cup sugar, to taste
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • ½ teaspoon cinnamon or a grating of nutmeg
  • A pinch of salt
  1. Press the pulp through a coarse sieve or pick out every seed by hand. Seeds should not be crushed or eaten.
  2. Whisk the eggs, milk, sugar, vanilla, spice, and salt, then whisk in the pulp.
  3. Pour into a buttered baking dish. Bake at 350°F until just set in the center, often 35–50 minutes depending on the dish. Cool somewhat before serving.

From the field: How to find pawpaws. Pulp freezes well if you want to cook later. For other fruit, see jams and preserves.

Spring · Serves 2

Spring violet greens

Young violet leaves have a mild, slightly mucilaginous texture and can be treated like a small salad green or wilted in a pan. Flowers, if you take only a few from a generous patch, can go on at the end. Use only plants you are sure are wild violets, from unsprayed ground.

Ingredients

  • A few generous handfuls of young violet leaves, well washed
  • A small handful of violet flowers, optional
  • A little olive oil or butter
  • Salt, and a squeeze of lemon
  1. Pick over the leaves and discard any that are yellowed, dirty, or unsure.
  2. For a salad, dry them well, toss with oil, lemon, and salt, and scatter on the flowers.
  3. For a wilt, warm the butter or oil and cook the leaves just until they soften, a minute or two. Season, and add flowers off the heat if you are using them.

From the field: How to find violets · Flowers also belong in syrup or vinegar.

Fall through spring · About 1 cup

Wild onion butter

When you are certain you have a wild onion or field garlic — crush a leaf; it must smell clearly of onion or garlic — a handful of greens can be worked into butter the same way as ramp leaves. If the smell is missing or you are unsure, do not use the plant.

Ingredients

  • 1 stick (8 tablespoons) unsalted butter, soft
  • A small handful of well-washed wild onion or field garlic greens, finely chopped
  • A pinch of salt
  1. Dry the greens thoroughly and chop them fine.
  2. Work them into the butter with salt until the flecks are even.
  3. Refrigerate and use within a week, or freeze in small portions. Use it on bread, potatoes, or eggs.

From the field: How to find wild garlic and onion · Related: ramp leaf butter.

Summer · Serves 2

Skillet chanterelles

Chanterelles are mostly water at first. A dry pan, then butter, is a simple way to cook them through without leaving them soggy. Use only mushrooms you have identified with certainty, and cook them thoroughly.

Ingredients

  • A generous handful of cleaned chanterelles, halved if large
  • 1 to 2 tablespoons butter
  • Salt, and a little pepper or parsley if you like
  • A small minced shallot, optional
  1. Brush off debris; avoid soaking them if you can. Discard any that are old, soggy, or unsure.
  2. Warm a skillet over medium heat and cook the chanterelles dry until they give off moisture and it mostly cooks away.
  3. Add the butter and shallot, and cook until the mushrooms are tender and beginning to brown, often another 5–8 minutes. Season and serve.

From the field: How to find chanterelles · They also dry well.

Early summer · Serves 4

Blackberry skillet crisp

Ripe wild blackberries need very little help. This is a simple baked crisp for a couple of pints of fruit you are sure of, picked from unsprayed ground and fully black and soft.

Ingredients

  • About 4 cups ripe blackberries
  • 2 to 3 tablespoons sugar, depending on the fruit
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice
  • ½ cup rolled oats, ⅓ cup flour, ⅓ cup brown sugar, a pinch of salt, and 4 tablespoons cold butter, for the topping
  1. Heat the oven to 375°F. Toss the berries with sugar and lemon in a small skillet or baking dish.
  2. Rub the topping ingredients together until crumbly and scatter them over the fruit.
  3. Bake until the top is browned and the fruit is bubbling, often 25–35 minutes. Cool somewhat before serving.

From the field: How to find blackberries · Extra fruit can go into jam.

Late fall · Serves 6

Persimmon pudding

A straightforward baked pudding for fully ripe American persimmon pulp — fruit that is soft all the way through. If the pulp still tastes astringent, the fruit was not ready. Do not use the seeds.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups seeded, ripe persimmon pulp
  • 2 eggs, 1 cup milk, ½ cup sugar
  • 1 cup flour, 1 teaspoon baking powder, ½ teaspoon cinnamon, a pinch of salt
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter
  1. Heat the oven to 350°F and butter a baking dish.
  2. Whisk the pulp, eggs, milk, sugar, and butter, then stir in the dry ingredients just until combined.
  3. Bake until the center is set, often 40–55 minutes. Serve warm.

From the field: How to find persimmons · Pulp also freezes well.

Winter pantry · About 2 dozen

Black walnut cookies

Cured, carefully cracked black walnuts have a stronger flavor than English walnuts, so a modest amount goes a long way. Use only kernels that smell sweet and look sound.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour, ½ teaspoon baking soda, a pinch of salt
  • ½ cup butter, soft, and ½ cup brown sugar, plus 1 egg
  • ½ teaspoon vanilla
  • ½ cup chopped black walnut kernels
  1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Cream the butter and sugar, then beat in the egg and vanilla.
  2. Stir in the dry ingredients and the nuts.
  3. Drop spoonfuls onto a baking sheet and bake until just set and lightly browned at the edges, often 10–12 minutes.

From the field: Black walnuts · Husking and curing. Hickory nutmeats can stand in if that is what you have.

Fall and winter

Hickory nuts in the kitchen

Sweet hickory nutmeats — from a species you can actually name, such as shagbark — can be eaten as they are, folded into the cookie dough above, or scattered on oatmeal or roasted squash. The work is in the cracking, not in a complicated recipe.

Taste a piece first. If it is bitter, you may have a bitternut or a spoiled kernel; do not use the batch until you know which. Store picked-out nutmeats in the refrigerator or freezer.

Fall · Serves 2–3

Pan-seared hen of the woods

Young, clean hen of the woods tears into fronds that brown well in a skillet. As with other wild mushrooms, identification must be certain, the cooking must be thorough, and a first taste should be small.

Ingredients

  • About ¾ pound young hen of the woods, cleaned and torn into pieces
  • Salt and pepper
  • 2 tablespoons oil or butter
  • A little garlic or parsley, optional
  1. Trim away dirty or tough base. Tear into bite-size fronds and pat dry.
  2. Sear in a moderately hot skillet in a single layer until browned and cooked through, often 8–12 minutes. Season as they cook.
  3. Add garlic only at the end if you use it, so it does not burn.

From the field: How to find hen of the woods · Extra, if it was young and clean, can be dried.

Winter and early spring · Serves 2

Chickweed salad

Tender chickweed tips, from plants you are sure of, make a mild green salad in the months when the garden is mostly asleep. Wash it well; it grows close to the ground.

Ingredients

  • A few handfuls of clean chickweed tips
  • A little oil, lemon or vinegar, and salt
  • Optional: a sliced radish or a hard-boiled egg
  1. Wash the greens in several changes of water and dry them thoroughly.
  2. Toss with oil, a little acid, and salt just before you eat. Add the extras if you have them.

From the field: How to find chickweed · Best used fresh.

Winter pantry · Serves 2–3

Acorn meal cakes

These small cakes start from acorn meal that has already been leached until it no longer tastes bitter. If the meal is still astringent, it is not ready — go back to the leaching step. This is not a recipe for raw acorns.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup thoroughly leached, dried acorn meal
  • ½ cup wheat flour (or another flour you bake with), ½ teaspoon baking powder, a pinch of salt
  • 1 egg, ½ cup milk, and 1 tablespoon oil or melted butter
  1. Stir the dry ingredients, then the wet, into a thick batter. Let it stand a few minutes.
  2. Cook small cakes on a lightly oiled griddle or skillet over medium heat until set and browned on both sides.
  3. Serve warm. If the cakes taste bitter, the meal was not leached enough; do not keep eating them.

From the field: Acorns · Leaching and meal.

Winter · Serves 2

Sautéed oyster mushrooms

Torn oyster mushrooms brown quickly in a hot pan. Use only a sure identification, discard slimy or aging clusters, and cook them all the way through. A first portion should be small.

Ingredients

  • A generous handful of clean oyster mushrooms, torn into pieces
  • 1 to 2 tablespoons butter or oil
  • Salt, and a little garlic or parsley if you like
  1. Trim the tough attachment at the wood. Tear into strips and pat dry.
  2. Sear in a moderately hot pan until browned and cooked through, often 6–10 minutes. Season as they cook.
  3. Add garlic only at the end if you use it.

From the field: How to find oyster mushrooms · Clean extras can be dried.
