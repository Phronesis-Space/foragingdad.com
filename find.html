Identification, seasons, and place

How to find them

A field guide to a handful of well-known wild foods of the Southeast, written with East Tennessee in mind: cove hardwoods, oak ridges, river bottoms, and field edges. It is a starting point for study, not a license to eat something you cannot name with certainty.

Read this first

Never eat a wild plant or mushroom unless you are 100% sure of the identification. A close enough guess is not enough. Learn lookalikes, use more than one feature, and get a second opinion from a trusted guide or a local expert when you are new to a species.

Permission and land rules matter as much as identification. Foraging is generally prohibited in Great Smoky Mountains National Park and in most Tennessee state parks. National forests and other public lands have their own, changing rules. Private land requires the landowner’s permission. Check current regulations before you go, and do not trespass.

Spring ephemeral · Allium

Ramps

Allium tricoccum — also called wild leeks

Season in East Tennessee
March through early May, often before the canopy fully closes. The broad leaves come first; flowers appear later, after the leaves have usually faded.
Where to look
Moist, rich cove hardwoods and north-facing slopes — maple, beech, tulip poplar, and similar woods, often near streams. In East Tennessee they are more reliable in the foothills and mountains than on dry ridges. Take leaves sparingly and leave the bulbs, especially in a small patch. Ramps are slow to recover from heavy digging.
Identification tip
Broad, smooth, tulip-shaped leaves, often with a reddish or burgundy lower stem. Crush a leaf: it must smell strongly of onion or garlic. If it does not, it is not a ramp. Do not taste plants to identify them.
Lookalikes and cautions
Lily of the valley, false hellebore, fly poison, and other lily-family plants can share the same woods and do not smell of onion. Some of those plants are toxic. Smell is required, but smell alone is not a complete identification — learn the whole plant.

In the kitchen: Ramp leaf butter · Pickling

Spring · Toothsome and short-lived

Morels

Morchella species — yellow and black morels both occur in the region

Season in East Tennessee
Usually late March through May in East Tennessee, after warm rains, often around the time of redbuds and mayapples. A cold snap can pause a flush; a hot, dry spell can end it.
Where to look
Dying or dead elm, ash, old apple, tulip poplar, and disturbed or burned ground are frequent places to look, but morels are unpredictable. Search slowly, close to the ground. Do not collect on land where it is not allowed.
Identification tip
The cap is covered in pits and ridges, not wrinkled lobes. When you cut a true morel lengthwise, it is hollow from the tip of the cap through the stem. Learn the difference between yellow morels, black morels, and half-free morels from a dedicated guide.
Lookalikes and cautions
False morels (including Gyromitra and some Verpa) can look similar at a glance. Their caps are often brain-like or draped, and the interior is not a single clean hollow. Some are seriously toxic. If you cannot cut it and see a hollow true morel, leave it. Always cook morels thoroughly; never eat them raw.

In the kitchen: Skillet morels on toast · Drying

Summer · After the rains

Chanterelles

Cantharellus species — several gold and pinkish chanterelles grow in the Southeast, including the smooth chanterelle

Season in East Tennessee
June into September, sometimes later, typically after good summer rains.
Where to look
On the ground in mixed woods, often near oaks or pines, scattered or in loose troops — not in tight, overlapping shelves attached to wood. Look on mossy banks and open forest soil, not on logs.
Identification tip
The underside has blunt, forked ridges (false gills) that run down the stem, not sharp, knife-like gills. Flesh is usually pale. Many have a faint fruity, apricot-like smell. They grow from soil, not as a dense cluster on a stump.
Lookalikes and cautions
Jack-o’-lantern mushrooms (Omphalotus illudens) are toxic and grow in tight clusters on wood or buried wood, with true sharp gills. They can be orange and convincing at a glance. False chanterelles have true gills and a different construction. If it is growing like a shelf on a log, it is not a chanterelle. Cook thoroughly; some people still get stomach upset.

In the kitchen: Drying · Use only a sure identification before any recipe.

Summer and fall · Shelves on wood

Chicken of the woods

Laetiporus species — in the East, often L. sulphureus (yellow pores) or L. cincinnatus (white pores, often at the base of oak)

Season in East Tennessee
Late spring through fall, with flushes in warm, wet weather. Peak is often summer into early autumn.
Where to look
On hardwoods, especially oak: standing trunks, stumps, or at the base of a tree. Note the tree; do not take old, sour, or crumbling shelves, and leave enough of a young flush that it can still be recognized as a living fungus.
Identification tip
Overlapping, soft-edged shelves in bright orange and yellow, with a pore surface underneath — not gills. Young growth is flexible and moist; the tender edge is the part people cook.
Lookalikes and cautions
Several bracket fungi can look vaguely similar. Learn the pore surface and color of the species in this region. Some people are sensitive even to correctly identified chicken of the woods, especially from unusual host trees. Avoid old specimens. Cook thoroughly and try a little first. If the mushroom smells sour or feels chalky and dry, leave it.

In the kitchen: Pan-seared chicken of the woods · Drying

Late summer into fall

Lion’s mane

Hericium erinaceus — related Hericium species with branched or comb-like spines also grow here

Season in East Tennessee
Often August through November in East Tennessee, after cool rains, sometimes later in a mild fall.
Where to look
On wounds, scars, and dead wood of hardwoods such as oak, beech, and maple — on standing trees or fallen logs. Look up as well as down.
Identification tip
A white, rounded mass of hanging, icicle-like spines with no cap and no gills. Fresh specimens are white to creamy, not orange, brown, or slimy. Related Hericium species may be more branched; learn those forms from a mushroom guide before you eat them.
Lookalikes and cautions
A classic lion’s mane is fairly distinctive, but “fairly distinctive” is not the same as a sure identification. Do not eat aging, discolored, or waterlogged specimens. Cook thoroughly. As with other wild mushrooms, try a small amount first.

In the kitchen: Drying · A simple sear in butter, like other tender mushrooms, is enough once identification is certain.

Early fall fruit · Native tree

Pawpaw

Asimina triloba — the pawpaw of Eastern river bottoms

Season in East Tennessee
Fruit ripens in a short window, usually September into October. Flowers are maroon and appear in spring; the large, tropical-looking leaves are present all summer.
Where to look
Understory of rich, moist bottomlands and creek sides, often in colonies of several trees. In the Knoxville area they are a native tree of river woods, not a mountain-ridge plant. Ripe fruit is soft, blotched, and strongly fragrant; it falls or pulls away easily. Unripe fruit is hard and green and should be left.
Identification tip
The fruit is oblong, a few inches long, green to yellow with brown freckling when ripe. Inside is a custard-yellow pulp and several large brown seeds. The leaves are long, simple, and tapered, and a broken twig has a characteristic smell some people compare to peppers or solvents.
Lookalikes and cautions
Eat only ripe pulp. Do not eat or crush the seeds, and most people discard the skin. Unripe fruit can be unpleasant. Some people have stomach upset or other sensitivities; try a little first. This is a fruit identification, not a lookalike-heavy mushroom problem, but unripe or misidentified fruit is still not food.

In the kitchen: Simple pawpaw pudding · Pulp also freezes well. See preserves.

Early to midsummer · Cane fruit

Blackberries

Rubus species — several native and naturalized blackberries and related cane fruits grow in Tennessee

Season in East Tennessee
Typically June and July in East Tennessee, a little later at elevation. Fruit is ripe when it is dull black and lets go of the plant with a light pull.
Where to look
Sunny edges, old fields, logging roads, fencerows, and woodland openings. Wear long sleeves; the canes are thorny. Do not pick along roads or fields that may have been sprayed, and leave plenty of ripe fruit for wildlife.
Identification tip
An aggregate fruit (a cluster of tiny drupelets) on a thorny cane, ripening from green to red to black. Ripe blackberries are dull, not shiny like some unripe fruit, and they come off easily. Learn the difference between blackberries, dewberries, and wineberries if you want a precise name; many Rubus fruits are eaten when fully ripe, but you should still know what you are picking.
Lookalikes and cautions
Red, unripe fruit is not at its best and is easy to confuse if you are not looking closely. Avoid dusty or sprayed roadsides. If you have any doubt that the plant is a Rubus, do not eat it.

In the kitchen: Jams and preserves

Cool months · The onion-smell rule

Wild garlic and wild onion

Allium vineale (field garlic) and Allium canadense (meadow garlic), among other wild Alliums

Season in East Tennessee
Green shoots are often present from late fall through spring in East Tennessee, including mild winters. They are one of the few wild flavors still standing after frost.
Where to look
Lawns, fields, roadsides, meadow edges, and open woods. Field garlic is a common naturalized plant; meadow garlic is native. Skip ground that may have been treated with herbicides, and be cautious of polluted roadsides.
Identification tip
Leaves are slender and hollow or grass-like, depending on the species, and the plant forms bulbs. Crush a leaf or bulb: it must smell clearly of onion or garlic. No onion or garlic smell means you do not have an Allium. Do not taste plants to identify them.
Lookalikes and cautions
This is one of the more serious lookalike groups. Death camas, crow poison, and some other lily-family plants can grow in grassy or open places and can be toxic. They do not smell like onion or garlic, but relying on a quick glance is how people make mistakes. If the onion smell is absent, weak, or you are unsure, leave it in the ground.

In the kitchen: Infused vinegars · Pickling · A few chopped greens can stand in for chives when identification is certain.

Spring · Flowers and young leaves

Violets

Viola sororia — the common blue violet, and related wild violets

Season in East Tennessee
March through May, often in the same weeks as ramps and morels. Flowers and fresh young leaves are the usual harvest.
Where to look
Lawns, open woods, paths, and damp edges. They are ordinary spring plants in East Tennessee, not a rare mountain endemic. Skip sprayed lawns and roadsides.
Identification tip
Heart-shaped leaves with scalloped edges, and five-petaled flowers (commonly violet-blue, sometimes white or bicolor) with a slightly irregular face. Learn the plant as a whole. Garden African violets are unrelated houseplants and are not this species.
Lookalikes and cautions
Be sure you have a wild violet (Viola). Do not assume every purple flower in spring is edible. Use modest amounts the first time; some people find the leaves mucilaginous or mildly irritating in quantity. This is not a lookalike-heavy mushroom problem, but identification still has to be certain before you eat it.

In the kitchen: Spring violet greens · Flowers can also be used in a violet syrup or vinegar.

Fall · Nut tree

Black walnuts

Juglans nigra — the black walnut of Eastern woods and farm edges

Season in East Tennessee
Nuts drop in September and October. The work of husking, curing, and cracking usually stretches into late fall and winter.
Where to look
Bottomland woods, old fields, fencerows, and open hardwoods across East Tennessee. The tree is tall, with compound leaves and a distinctive aromatic smell when the husk or a twig is bruised.
Identification tip
A round green husk, staining yellowish-brown as it ages, around a deeply grooved dark shell that is harder than an English walnut. The kernel is rich and strongly flavored. Wear gloves when husking — the juice stains skin, clothes, and pavement.
Lookalikes and cautions
Do not confuse the tree with lookalike walnuts or hickories if you are not sure; the husk, shell, and leaf together make a sure identification. Never eat moldy or rancid kernels. Give freshly hulled nuts time to cure so the flavor settles.

In the kitchen: Husking and curing · Black walnut cookies.

Fall · Native fruit

Persimmons

Diospyros virginiana — the American persimmon, not the large grocery-store Asian fruit

Season in East Tennessee
Fruit ripens in October and November, often after cool nights or a frost. It is ready only when it is fully soft and ready to let go of the tree.
Where to look
Field edges, old home sites, open woods, and bottomlands. The trees are often modest, with blocky dark bark. Wildlife gets there early; what you find on the ground can still be good if it is clean and sound.
Identification tip
A small orange fruit, usually one to two inches, with a persistent four-lobed calyx at the stem end. Unripe fruit is firm and fiercely astringent. Ripe fruit is jelly-soft, sweet, and almost custardy. That change is the identification of readiness, not a suggestion to try a firm one “to see.”
Lookalikes and cautions
An unripe American persimmon will pucker your mouth unpleasantly. Do not eat the seeds. If you are looking at a large, squat grocery persimmon (Diospyros kaki), that is a different species with its own ripening rules. Be sure of both the plant and the ripeness.

In the kitchen: Pulp and putting up · Persimmon pudding.

Fall · Nut tree

Hickory nuts

Carya species — shagbark (C. ovata) and mockernut are the usual sweet nuts in this region

Season in East Tennessee
Nuts ripen and drop in September and October, and like black walnuts they are often cracked and eaten later.
Where to look
Upland oak–hickory woods, ridges, and coves throughout East Tennessee. Shagbark is named for its long, peeling plates of bark.
Identification tip
A four-valved husk that splits away from a hard, angled nut. Shagbark husks split cleanly to the base; the kernel is sweet. Learn bark, husk, and nut together.
Lookalikes and cautions
Bitternut hickory (Carya cordiformis) has a thinner, yellowish bud and a husk that does not split all the way; the nut is bitter and not a good edible. If you cannot tell a sweet hickory from a bitter one, do not eat it. As with all nuts, discard moldy kernels.

Fall · At the foot of oak

Hen of the woods

Grifola frondosa — also called maitake

Season in East Tennessee
Usually September through November in East Tennessee, after cool rains, at the base of living or dead oaks.
Where to look
The base of oak trees, in a clustered rosette rather than a single shelf high on a trunk. The same tree may fruit again in later years. Do not forage in the National Park or in parks that prohibit collecting.
Identification tip
Many overlapping, gray-brown leafy lobes from a single branching base, with a white pore surface underneath — no gills. The whole cluster can be large. Flesh should be clean and not sour or crumbling.
Lookalikes and cautions
Other clustered polypores grow at the base of trees. Learn the branching structure and pore surface from a dedicated mushroom guide. Cook thoroughly, and try a small amount first; some people get stomach upset from even a correctly identified specimen. Leave old, insect-riddled, or off-smelling clusters.

In the kitchen: Drying · Pan-seared hen of the woods.

Winter and early spring · Tender green

Chickweed

Stellaria media — common chickweed

Season in East Tennessee
In East Tennessee it often stays green through mild winters and puts on growth whenever the weather eases, then again in early spring. A hard freeze may set it back, but it frequently returns.
Where to look
Gardens, paths, field edges, and open disturbed ground. It likes cool, slightly damp soil and is often underfoot rather than deep in the woods.
Identification tip
Opposite leaves, a single line of fine hairs along the stem, and tiny white flowers whose five petals are so deeply notched that they often look like ten. A careful break in the stem can show a thin, elastic inner thread. Learn all of those features together.
Lookalikes and cautions
Scarlet pimpernel and some other low greens can share the same ground and are not chickweed. If the flower, the hair line, and the leaf arrangement do not add up, leave it. Harvest clean plants from unsprayed ground, wash them well, and try a little first.

In the kitchen: Best used fresh · Chickweed salad.

Fall drop, winter pantry

Acorns

Quercus species — white oak group acorns are generally the easier starting point

Season in East Tennessee
Acorns drop in autumn. As a food they are usually stored and leached, then used in the cold months. Midwinter “foraging” for acorns is often a matter of using what you already put up, or of finding sound fallen nuts that have not molded or sprouted badly.
Where to look
Oak woods throughout East Tennessee. White oaks (rounded leaf lobes, acorns that mature in one season) are the traditional choice for food because they tend to have less tannin than the red oak group.
Identification tip
An acorn is a nut in a scaly cap. Learn the oak that produced it. Sound nuts are heavy for their size, free of holes, and not moldy. Float tests and careful sorting help, but they do not replace a good look at the kernel.
Lookalikes and cautions
Raw unleached acorns are bitter with tannins and should not be eaten as a snack. Leaching is not optional. Discard moldy, rancid, or insect-ruined nuts. If you cannot tell a sound kernel from a bad one, do not use it. This is a processed food, not a nibble off the forest floor.

In the kitchen: Leaching and meal · Acorn meal cakes.

Cool weather · Dead hardwoods

Oyster mushrooms

Pleurotus ostreatus and close relatives — the cool-weather oysters of Eastern hardwoods

Season in East Tennessee
Late fall through winter in East Tennessee, and sometimes again in cool, wet spells. They often flush after rain on cold days when few other mushrooms are out.
Where to look
Dead or dying hardwood logs, stumps, and wounds — shelving in clusters, not growing as a toadstool from soil. Look at eye level on logs as well as on the ground.
Identification tip
A soft, oyster- or fan-shaped cap, usually pale, with gills that run down a short, often off-center stem. The flesh is white. Some have a faint anise or oyster-like smell. They grow on wood, in clusters.
Lookalikes and cautions
Several pale wood mushrooms can look similar, and some are not edible. Learn the gills, the stem, the habitat, and a spore print from a dedicated guide. Do not eat a “probable” oyster. Avoid old, slimy, or orange-stained specimens. Cook thoroughly, and try a small amount first.
